Adapted from UCL's HPSC0004 Philosophy of Science 1 (2023/24, Jubber and Tobin). Changes from the original: *Theory and Reality* replaces Ladyman as the main text; explanation and understanding are expanded from one week to four; the philosophy-of-technology week is replaced by a week on data, computation, and opacity; a week on discovery is added; the values week is reduced to inductive risk and folded into the week on evidence. Fourteen weeks, one topic per week. Required readings are the original module's seminar readings plus a few additions, and are short primary sources. Optional readings are for weeks that touch the bet directly. Three 2000-word essays, at the end of each block. Exam-style questions are set each week and kept off this page.

Main text throughout: Godfrey-Smith, P. *Theory and Reality: An Introduction to the Philosophy of Science*. Chapter numbers differ between the 2003 and 2021 editions, so chapters are given by title. The 2021 edition has a chapter on models; use it in week 7.

## Block I: Knowledge and method

## Week 1: Empiricism, induction, verificationism

Main text: "Logic Plus Empiricism" and "Induction and Confirmation."

Required:

1. Ayer, A. J. (1936). "The Elimination of Metaphysics." *Language, Truth and Logic*, ch. 1.
2. Hume, D. (1748). *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, §§2–4.
3. Goodman, N. (1955). "The New Riddle of Induction." *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*, ch. 3.

Optional:

1. Henderson, L. "The Problem of Induction." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.
2. Friedman, M. (1999). *Reconsidering Logical Positivism*, introduction.

For the bet: Goodman's riddle is the inductive-bias problem stated in 1955. What plays the role of entrenchment in a trained model?

## Week 2: Falsificationism, underdetermination, experiment

Main text: "Popper: Conjecture and Refutation."

Required:

1. Popper, K. (1963). "Science: Conjectures and Refutations." *Conjectures and Refutations*, ch. 1.
2. Quine, W. V. O. (1951). "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." *Philosophical Review* 60(1): 20–43.
3. Duhem, P. (1906). "Physical Theory and Experiment." *The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory*, part II, ch. 6.

Optional:

1. Stanford, K. "Underdetermination of Scientific Theory." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.
2. Jeffrey, R. (1975). "Probability and Falsification: Critique of the Popper Program." *Synthese* 30: 95–117.
3. Grünbaum, A. (1976). "Is the Method of Bold Conjectures and Attempted Refutations Justifiably the Method of Science?" *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 27(2): 105–136.

For the bet: this is the week to read Deutsch's Popper against Popper. Where does Deutsch go beyond him?

## Week 3: Kuhn, normal science, revolutions

Main text: "Kuhn and Normal Science," "Kuhn and Scientific Revolutions," and "Lakatos, Laudan, Feyerabend, and Frameworks."

Required:

1. Kuhn, T. S. (1962). *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, chs. 2, 7, 9.
2. Longino, H. (2012). "Lessons from Teaching *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*." *Historical Studies in the Natural Sciences* 42(5): 542–544.

Optional:

1. Masterman, M. (1970). "The Nature of a Paradigm." In Lakatos and Musgrave (eds.), *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge*, 59–90.
2. Popper, K. (1970). "Normal Science and Its Dangers." Same volume, 51–58.
3. Bird, A. "Thomas Kuhn." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.
4. Kitcher, P. (2012). "The Many Lessons of *Structure*." *Historical Studies in the Natural Sciences* 42(5): 255–282.

For the bet: Kuhn's account of how a field acquires a paradigm is the closest thing philosophy has to a theory of "what makes a field a field." Does AI for Science have one, and what would it take to get one?

## Block II: Realism

## Week 4: Realism, the no-miracles argument, the pessimistic induction

Main text: "Scientific Realism."

Required:

1. Laudan, L. (1981). "A Confutation of Convergent Realism." *Philosophy of Science* 48(1): 19–49.
2. Musgrave, A. (1988). "The Ultimate Argument for Scientific Realism." In Nola (ed.), *Relativism and Realism in Science*.

Optional:

1. Putnam, H. (1976). "What is 'Realism'?" *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 76: 177–194.
2. Psillos, S. (1999). *Scientific Realism: How Science Tracks Truth*, chs. 4–5.
3. Chakravartty, A. "Scientific Realism." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.

For the bet: Laudan's list is a list of theories that predicted well and were wrong about what exists. What is the analogue for a model that predicts well?

## Week 5: Constructive empiricism, entity realism

Main text: "Scientific Realism" (second half) and "Empiricism, Naturalism, and Scientific Realism?"

Required:

1. van Fraassen, B. (1980). "Arguments Concerning Scientific Realism." *The Scientific Image*, ch. 2.
2. Hacking, I. (1983). "Experimentation and Scientific Realism." *Representing and Intervening*, ch. 16.

Optional:

1. Ladyman, J. (2000). "What's Really Wrong with Constructive Empiricism? Van Fraassen and the Metaphysics of Modality." *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 51(4): 837–856.
2. Miller, B. (2016). "What Is Hacking's Argument for Entity Realism?" *Synthese* 193: 991–1006.
3. Cartwright, N. (1983). "When Explanation Leads to Inference." *How the Laws of Physics Lie*, essay 5.
4. Hacking, I. (1989). "Extragalactic Reality: The Case of Gravitational Lensing." *Philosophy of Science* 56(4): 555–581.

For the bet: constructive empiricism is the strongest philosophical version of "prediction is enough." Entity realism says belief follows from the ability to intervene. Which of the two describes what you actually believe about a trained model?

**Essay 1 (end of week 5).** Does the predictive success of a machine-learning model give us reason to believe it has captured the structure of the world? Answer with reference to the no-miracles argument, the pessimistic induction, and constructive empiricism. 2000 words.

## Block III: Evidence and models

## Week 6: Evidence: Bayesian confirmation and inductive risk

Main text: "Bayesianism and Modern Theories of Evidence."

Required:

1. Strevens, M. *Notes on Bayesian Confirmation Theory*, §§1–5 (lecture notes, NYU).
2. Gelman, A. and Shalizi, C. (2013). "Philosophy and the Practice of Bayesian Statistics." *British Journal of Mathematical and Statistical Psychology* 66(1): 8–38.
3. Douglas, H. (2000). "Inductive Risk and Values in Science." *Philosophy of Science* 67(4): 559–579.

Optional:

1. Norton, J. (2003). "A Material Theory of Induction." *Philosophy of Science* 70(4): 647–670.
2. Mayo, D. (2018). *Statistical Inference as Severe Testing*, excursion 1.
3. Longino, H. (1990). "Values and Objectivity." *Science as Social Knowledge*, ch. 4.

For the bet: Deutsch rejects Bayesian epistemology outright. Gelman and Shalizi are Bayesians who agree with Popper about testing. Where exactly do they disagree with Deutsch?

## Week 7: Models and idealization

Main text: the models chapter (2021 edition), or, failing that, Frigg and Hartmann, "Models in Science," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.

Required:

1. Elgin, C. (2004). "True Enough." *Philosophical Issues* 14: 113–131.
2. Weisberg, M. (2007). "Three Kinds of Idealization." *Journal of Philosophy* 104(12): 639–659.
3. Parker, W. (2020). "Model Evaluation: An Adequacy-for-Purpose View." *Philosophy of Science* 87(3): 457–477.
4. Leonelli, S. (2019). "What Distinguishes Data from Models?" *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* 9(2): 22.
5. Morrison, M. and Morgan, M. (1999). "Models as Mediating Instruments." In Morgan and Morrison (eds.), *Models as Mediators*.

Optional:

1. Cartwright, N. (1983). *How the Laws of Physics Lie*, introduction and essay 3.
2. Potochnik, A. (2017). *Idealization and the Aims of Science*, chs. 1–2.
3. Alexandrova, A. (2008). "Making Models Count." *Philosophy of Science* 75(3): 383–404.
4. Currie, A. (2015). "Philosophy of Science and the Curse of the Case Study." In Daly (ed.), *The Palgrave Handbook of Philosophical Methods*.

For the bet: Parker says a model is evaluated by adequacy for a purpose, not by truth. Is a foundation model adequate for the purpose of understanding, and who decides?

## Week 8: Data, phenomena, computation, opacity

No main-text chapter.

Required:

1. Bogen, J. and Woodward, J. (1988). "Saving the Phenomena." *Philosophical Review* 97(3): 303–352.
2. Humphreys, P. (2009). "The Philosophical Novelty of Computer Simulation Methods." *Synthese* 169(3): 615–626.
3. Creel, K. (2020). "Transparency in Complex Computational Systems." *Philosophy of Science* 87(4): 568–589.
4. Boge, F. (2022). "Two Dimensions of Opacity and the Deep Learning Predicament." *Minds and Machines* 32: 43–75.

Optional:

1. Humphreys, P. (2004). *Extending Ourselves: Computational Science, Empiricism, and Scientific Method*, chs. 1, 5.
2. Winsberg, E. (2010). *Science in the Age of Computer Simulation*, chs. 1–2.
3. Boon, M. (2015). "The Scientific Use of Technological Instruments." In Hansson (ed.), *The Role of Technology in Science*.

For the bet: Bogen and Woodward say theories explain phenomena, not data. A model is trained on data. What does it take for a model to be about a phenomenon?

**Essay 2 (end of week 8).** "All models are wrong, but some are useful." What, if anything, does a model's usefulness tell us about its truth? Answer with reference to idealization and adequacy-for-purpose, using a trained neural network as your central example. 2000 words.

## Block IV: Explanation and understanding

## Week 9: Explanation I: covering law, unification, pragmatics

Main text: "Explanation."

Required:

1. Hempel, C. and Oppenheim, P. (1948). "Studies in the Logic of Explanation." *Philosophy of Science* 15(2): 135–175, parts I–III.
2. Friedman, M. (1974). "Explanation and Scientific Understanding." *Journal of Philosophy* 71(1): 5–19.
3. Kitcher, P. (1981). "Explanatory Unification." *Philosophy of Science* 48(4): 507–531.
4. van Fraassen, B. (1977). "The Pragmatics of Explanation." *American Philosophical Quarterly* 14(2): 143–150.

Optional:

1. Salmon, W. (1989). *Four Decades of Scientific Explanation*, introduction and ch. 1.
2. Woodward, J. and Ross, L. "Scientific Explanation." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.

For the bet: on Kitcher's account, to explain is to unify. Is "AI as the fabric connecting patchwork scientific principles" a unificationist claim, and if so, what is being unified?

## Week 10: Explanation II: intervention and mechanism

No main-text chapter.

Required:

1. Woodward, J. (2003). *Making Things Happen*, chs. 1–2.
2. Machamer, P., Darden, L., and Craver, C. (2000). "Thinking about Mechanisms." *Philosophy of Science* 67(1): 1–25.

Optional:

1. Woodward, J. "Causation and Manipulability." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.
2. Batterman, R. and Rice, C. (2014). "Minimal Model Explanations." *Philosophy of Science* 81(3): 349–376.
3. Strevens, M. (2008). *Depth: An Account of Scientific Explanation*, ch. 1.

For the bet: Woodward's account gives a test. A model explains if it answers what-if-things-had-been-different questions correctly under intervention. Can this be run on a trained model, and what would count as passing?

## Week 11: Understanding

No main-text chapter.

Required:

1. de Regt, H. and Dieks, D. (2005). "A Contextual Approach to Scientific Understanding." *Synthese* 144: 137–170.
2. de Regt, H. (2017). *Understanding Scientific Understanding*, chs. 1–2 and 4.
3. Strevens, M. (2013). "No Understanding without Explanation." *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science* 44(3): 510–515.
4. Lipton, P. (2009). "Understanding without Explanation." In de Regt, Leonelli, and Eigner (eds.), *Scientific Understanding: Philosophical Perspectives*.
5. Elgin, C. (2007). "Understanding and the Facts." *Philosophical Studies* 132(1): 33–42.

Optional:

1. Khalifa, K. (2017). *Understanding, Explanation, and Scientific Knowledge*, ch. 1.
2. Potochnik, A. (2017). *Idealization and the Aims of Science*, ch. 4.

For the bet: de Regt says understanding requires an intelligible theory, where intelligibility is the ability to reason qualitatively about consequences without calculating. Is that a property a computational object can have, or only a person?

## Week 12: Understanding from machine learning

No main-text chapter.

Required:

1. Sullivan, E. (2022). "Understanding from Machine Learning Models." *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 73(1): 109–133.
2. Krenn, M. et al. (2022). "On Scientific Understanding with Artificial Intelligence." *Nature Reviews Physics* 4: 761–769.
3. Duede, E. (2023). "Deep Learning Opacity in Scientific Discovery." *Philosophy of Science* 90(5): 1089–1099.

Optional:

1. Chirimuuta, M. (2021). "Prediction versus Understanding in Computationally Enhanced Neuroscience." *Synthese* 199: 767–790.
2. Kawamleh, S. (2021). "Can Machines Learn How Clouds Work? The Epistemic Implications of Machine Learning Methods in Climate Science." *Philosophy of Science* 88(5): 1008–1020.
3. Räz, T. and Beisbart, C. (2022). "The Importance of Understanding Deep Learning." *Erkenntnis*, online first.

For the bet: Sullivan argues that what blocks understanding from a model is not its opacity but "link uncertainty," how well the model is connected to the target system. If she is right, what should an AI-for-science project produce alongside the model?

**Essay 3 (end of week 12).** Can a model that no one can interpret provide scientific understanding? Answer with reference to at least two accounts of understanding, and say what your answer implies for AI in science. 2000 words.

## Block V: Discovery and closing

## Week 13: Discovery

No main-text chapter.

Required:

1. Reichenbach, H. (1938). *Experience and Prediction*, ch. 1, §1 (the context of discovery and the context of justification).
2. Hanson, N. R. (1958). *Patterns of Discovery*, ch. 4, "Theories."
3. Simon, H. (1973). "Does Scientific Discovery Have a Logic?" *Philosophy of Science* 40(4): 471–480.
4. Langley, P., Simon, H., Bradshaw, G., and Zytkow, J. (1987). *Scientific Discovery: Computational Explorations of the Creative Processes*, ch. 1.

Optional:

1. Schickore, J. "Scientific Discovery." *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.
2. Popper, K. (1959). *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, §§1–2.
3. Ludwig, J. and Mullainathan, S. (2024). "Machine Learning as a Tool for Hypothesis Generation." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 139(2): 751–827.

For the bet: Popper and Reichenbach say there is no logic of discovery. Simon says there is, and BACON was built to show it. Which side is the "autonomous scientist" bet on, and what did BACON fail to do?

## Week 14: Closing

No new reading. Reread Kuhn ch. 2, "The Route to Normal Science."

Write v2 of the bet using the vocabulary from the course, with a revision-log entry saying which weeks changed which claims. Write a first one-page answer to "what is AI for Science for?", naming the shared problem and the shared standard for solving it.
